# Chapter 12: Generative AI and Fashion Prompts

Generative AI looks like magic, but the principle behind it is an extension of the probabilistic thinking and relationship learning covered so far. This chapter covers how the probabilistic principles of generative AI can be connected to fashion planning and content production.

## The Nature of Generative AI

Large language models (LLMs) and image generation models seem to create like a person, but they are fundamentally systems that **probabilistically calculate the most likely next word or pixel** given a certain context. Given an input like "oversized trench coat, beige tone, minimal," the model generates its next output based on patterns that frequently appeared alongside this context in the vast amount of image and text data it was trained on.

Seen this way, generative AI connects to the probabilistic thinking covered in Chapter 7 and the relationship learning covered in Chapter 6. It isn't creating something random — it is calculating the "plausible" next step by following the probability distribution observed within the training data.

## The Combination of Relationships and Optimization

The process by which a generative model is created also follows the same principle of error optimization covered in Chapter 11. As the model learns the relationships between styles and keywords, it adjusts its parameters in the direction that reduces the error between its predicted output and the actual data. Repeating this process over a vast amount of images and text, the model learns, in great detail, the relationships around which silhouettes, colors, and materials tend to appear alongside the word "minimal."

In other words, generative AI is not a completely new principle — it can be seen as the result of the two ideas covered so far in this course, relationship learning and error optimization, combined on top of massive data and computational resources.

## Why Precise Prompts Are Needed

Once you understand that the model probabilistically calculates the next output, it also becomes natural to understand why the specificity of a prompt (instruction) has such a large effect on the result. A vague request like "draw pretty clothes" spreads the probability distribution wide, making the result hard to predict, and it can come out very differently each time.

By contrast, specifying mood, silhouette, material, and texture concretely narrows the probability distribution, raising the odds of getting a result close to the intended direction. For example, listing concrete conditions like "oversized silhouette, linen material, earth-tone color, minimal mood summer jacket" leads the model to generate a result close to the pattern in which these conditions appeared together. This is the same principle as "making vague requests concrete," covered in Chapter 4 — just as SQL needs clear conditions, generative AI needs clear conditions to produce the desired result.

| Vague prompt | "Draw pretty clothes" | Spread wide — very different results each time |
| Specific prompt | "Oversized silhouette, linen material, earth-tone color, minimal mood summer jacket" | Narrowly concentrated — results close to intent |

## Practical Generative AI Literacy

The most important attitude when bringing generative AI into practice is to understand AI **not as a machine that replaces the designer, but as a tool that widens the scope of exploration and quickly compares multiple drafts**.

- AI can generate a wide variety of variations in a short time, broadening the range of initial ideas.
- However, whether the generated result is actually manufacturable, and whether it matches the brand's identity, must be judged by a person.
- You must also understand the limitation that generative AI is stronger at newly recombining existing patterns than at producing a completely new aesthetic that wasn't in its training data.

Having this understanding allows generative AI to be used appropriately in practice, neither overtrusted nor underestimated.

## Practice Guide: Same Theme, Different Terminology

Even given the same seasonal theme (e.g., "urban autumn"), the resulting output can vary greatly depending on the fashion terminology used. Try comparing the results of entering a general expression like "casual autumn clothes" versus a more technical expression like "tailored silhouette, herringbone pattern, Earl Grey-toned wool blend outerwear."

This comparison lets you confirm two things. First, the more specific and technical the vocabulary used, the closer the result gets to the intended direction. Second, the difference in results is not because the AI "thought differently" creatively, but because the input words differ in the probability patterns with which they appeared together in the training data. This exercise helps in accepting generative AI not as a mysterious black box, but as a tool that can be understood through probabilistic principles.

## Real-World Case: Levi's and H&M's Use of Generative AI Models, and the Backlash

In 2023, Levi's announced a collaboration with AI studio Lalaland.ai to use AI-generated model images of various body types, ages, and skin tones in product photography. The intent was to let consumers with more diverse bodies see a model resembling themselves, without increasing actual photoshoots — but it drew fierce criticism for "simulating diversity with images instead of real diversity," and Levi's had to clarify that this was not meant to be a substitute for its diversity policy.

Around the same time, H&M announced an attempt to create "digital twins" based on the faces of about 30 actual contracted models for use in marketing, stating that it would grant the models themselves ownership of their AI avatars and the right to license them to other brands, and would disclose in the images that generative AI had been used. Even so, backlash continued from photographers, stylists, and related professions concerned about job losses.

Both cases show, alongside the potential uses of generative AI covered in this chapter, the ethical and social costs that must always be considered together in practice. The technical fact that you can obtain the image you want with a precise prompt, and the question of whose consent and compensation that technology is used with, are different issues — practical generative AI literacy must be able to handle both together.

## Chapter Summary

- Generative AI generates output by calculating the probability of the next word or pixel given a context.
- A generative model is the result of the principles of relationship learning (Chapter 6) and error optimization (Chapter 11) combined on massive data.
- A specific prompt narrows the probability distribution, raising the odds of getting output close to the desired result.
- Generative AI should be used as a tool that widens the scope of exploration and compares drafts, not as a tool that replaces the designer.
- The fact that the same theme produces different results depending on the terminology used is due to differences in probability patterns, not creative judgment.

## Explore Further: Assignments Using Generative AI

1. **Experiment with prompt refinement**: Enter a vague prompt like "design an autumn jacket" and a prompt that concretely specifies silhouette, material, color, and mood into a generative AI (an image generation AI or a text-based style description AI), and compare the results side by side. Record which added word changed the result the most.
2. **Create an ethics scenario for using AI-generated models**: Referring to the Levi's and H&M cases, ask a generative AI to "suggest what backlash could be expected if our brand used AI-generated models in marketing, and what policy (consent, compensation, disclosure requirements, etc.) would be needed to reduce it," and discuss whether the suggestion is actually feasible.
3. **Self-assess your generative AI literacy**: Ask a generative AI to "tell me about common mistakes fashion practitioners make when overtrusting or underestimating generative AI," check for yourself which mistakes you might be prone to making, and write your own checklist to prevent them.
