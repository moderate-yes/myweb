# Chapter 2: How Computers Remember Fashion

Computers cannot see clothes with eyes or touch them with hands. For a computer to handle the elegance of a silhouette or the feel of a fabric, all of this must be converted into a form that can be **compared and computed**. This chapter looks at what data structures represent the sensory subject of fashion inside a computer, and what can and cannot be done depending on how that representation is chosen.

## Fashion Embeddings and Digitization

Information such as texture, silhouette, color, and the nuance of a review is natural to people but unfamiliar to computers — it is **unstructured data**. Computers cannot understand this information as-is; instead, they convert each subject into coordinates made of numbers, called an **embedding**.

The core idea of an embedding is to place similar things at nearby numbers and different things at distant numbers. For example, a minimalist oversized shirt and a loose-fit blouse have nearby coordinates in style space, and are far from an ornate sequined dress. Once things are converted into coordinates, questions close to human sensory judgment — like "products similar to this one" or "a style this customer would like" — can be turned into a mathematical problem of computing distance.

Digitization is not simply saving a photo as a file. The key point is that it is **a process of reconstructing information into a comparable form**. The same dress, if it exists only as an image file, cannot automatically be compared with other dresses, but once converted into an embedding, its similarity to tens of thousands of products can be computed at once.

## Images, Text, and Numerical Data

Fashion data can be divided into two clearly distinct axes.

- **Unstructured data (images, text)**: Product photos, wear reviews, and styling feedback carry a rich amount of information, but computers cannot immediately use them in calculations. Expressions like "pretty" or "good fit" have different standards for different people.
- **Structured data (numbers, categories)**: Information already expressed as numbers or fixed categories — such as price, total length, shoulder width, and color code — can immediately be used in calculations like sorting, filtering, and aggregation as soon as it's stored.

The two types of data are not substitutes but **complements**. Measurement data alone cannot describe a "sophisticated fit," and review text alone makes accurate size comparison difficult. In practice, unstructured data is processed into embeddings or tags and placed alongside structured data, designed so both kinds of information can be referenced together.

## RDB: An Orderly World

A **Relational Database (RDB)** stores information in tables with rows and columns, and connects the tables to each other through keys. It is suited to data where **the shape of the items is predetermined and accuracy matters** — such as inventory quantity by SKU, the fabric and trim composition in a BOM (Bill of Materials), and payment records.

The strength of an RDB is consistency. You can enforce that the SKU column of an inventory table only ever contains values in a fixed format, and information spanning multiple tables — such as orders and payments — can be linked accurately without breaking rules. This is why RDBs are widely used as the foundation of logistics and settlement systems, where even a single inventory discrepancy is unacceptable.

## NoSQL: A Flexible World

On the other hand, data such as style tags, social feeds, and freeform styling notes left by customers — where **the shape of information carried by each item frequently differs** — is hard to force into a fixed table. Some products might have 5 material tags attached, while others have 20.

A **NoSQL database** flexibly stores data in document or key-value form to fit such situations. Adding a new attribute doesn't require changing the structure of the entire table, and it is also advantageous for rapidly accumulating large volumes of logs or event data. In exchange, its strict consistency guarantees can be relatively weaker than an RDB's, which makes it better suited to exploratory, frequently changing data rather than payment or inventory data where accuracy is the top priority.

## Graph DB: A World Where Relationships Are the Value

There is also information where **the connections between entities matter more than the entities themselves** — such as the match between body type and style, the preferences between customers and products, or the network between influencers and followers. Representing this kind of data in tables is inefficient, because confirming relationships requires repeatedly joining multiple tables.

A **Graph Database (Graph DB)** stores entities as nodes and relationships as edges between nodes, and specializes in questions that follow connections, such as "customers with similar taste to this customer" or "products frequently bought together with this product." This is why graph DBs are frequently used in recommendation engines and social network analysis.

The three storage approaches are each suited to different purposes; their strengths and weaknesses can be summarized as follows.

| | RDB | NoSQL | Graph DB |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Data shape | Fixed tables (rows/columns) | Flexible documents/key-value | Nodes and relationships (edges) |
| Strength | Accuracy, consistency | Scalability, flexible schema | Speed of relationship traversal |
| Suitable data | Inventory, payments, BOM | Tags, logs, social feeds | Recommendations, social networks |
| Weakness | Schema changes are cumbersome | Weaker strict consistency guarantees | Disadvantageous for large-scale aggregation |

## Fashion Business Case: Turning Clicks into a Recommendation Asset

On a fashion platform, each click, wishlist add, and cart add a customer leaves looks trivial on its own, but together they form a network of relationships between customers and products. Representing this network as a graph structure makes it possible to quickly find "other products also liked by customers who liked this product," which directly feeds into a recommendation list.

At the same time, product images and reviews are converted into embeddings and used to compute "products that look similar" and "products that received similar reviews," while structured price and inventory data is used to filter for which products can actually be recommended (whether in stock, whether shippable). In the end, a single recommendation screen is the result of an RDB's accuracy, NoSQL's flexibility, a Graph DB's relationships, and embedding similarity calculations all working together.

## Real-World Case: Zalando's Fashion DNA

Zalando researched and built a system that feeds product images and attribute information into an 8-layer convolutional neural network (CNN), converting each product into a coordinate vector called "Fashion DNA." In the process of training this network on tasks like predicting brand or silhouette, or predicting which customers would purchase a given product, it ends up producing embeddings that place similarly styled products at nearby coordinates.

The coordinates created this way become the backbone of a recommendation system. Computing the distance between two products' coordinates allows quickly finding "products similar to this product," and comparing a customer's past purchase coordinates with product coordinates allows computing "products this customer would likely enjoy." This is a case that shows how the embedding concept explained in this chapter is actually implemented at a large fashion platform.

## Chapter Summary

- Computers cannot handle sensory information directly, so they convert it into comparable numbers — embeddings.
- Unstructured data (images, text) is information-rich but requires processing, while structured data (numbers, categories) is simpler but can be computed on immediately.
- RDBs are suited to data with a clear structure where accuracy matters (inventory, payments).
- NoSQL flexibly stores data whose shape changes frequently (tags, logs, feeds).
- Graph DBs are suited to data where the relationships between entities are themselves the value (recommendations, networks).
- Real-world data systems often use several structures together depending on purpose, rather than relying on a single storage method.
