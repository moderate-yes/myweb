# Chapter 7: Probabilistic Thinking

Perfectly predicting fashion trends is close to impossible. If so, the goal of a person working with data should not be accurate prediction, but rather **avoiding big failures amid uncertainty**. This chapter covers how to use probability not as a tool for hitting the trend, but as a language for risk management.

## A Defensive Strategy for Being Less Wrong

Many people expect data analysis to "precisely pinpoint next season's blockbuster product." But fashion demand has too many variables and too much volatility for any model to guarantee 100% accurate prediction.

Probabilistic thinking changes the approach itself. Instead of worrying about "what will be right," you also calculate "how much you lose when you're wrong." This is closer to a **defensive strategy that guards against losing big**, rather than an aggressive strategy of nailing the trend. Probability is not a crystal ball that tells the future, but a scale that lets you compare the risk of each option.

## A Portfolio That Doesn't Lose

Just as investing spreads money across multiple assets, product planning in fashion is safer with a **portfolio strategy that spreads risk across multiple products** rather than making one big bet.

- **Basic products**: Products with stable demand that is relatively easy to forecast. Low probability of failure, but big successes are also rare.
- **Trend products**: Products that could be a huge hit, or could be completely ignored. High volatility.

Building a lineup of only basics is stable but misses growth opportunities, while building a lineup of only trend products increases both the chance of big success and the risk of big loss. Mixing stable basics with adventurous trend products appropriately means that even if several trend products fail, basic sales can support overall performance, lowering the probability of failure for the whole portfolio.

| | Basic products | Trend products |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Difficulty of demand forecasting | Easy | Hard |
| Probability of failure | Low | High |
| Expected gain on success | Small | Large |
| Role in the portfolio | Stable revenue base | Securing growth opportunity |

## Expected Value and Production Volume

The tool that turns probabilistic thinking into actual numbers is **expected value**. Expected value is the sum of each outcome's probability multiplied by the size of that outcome.

$$
\text{Expected value} = \sum (\text{probability} \times \text{outcome})
$$

For example, if a trend product has a 70% chance of generating a profit of 50 million KRW and a 30% chance of a loss of 20 million KRW, the expected value is calculated as follows.

$$
0.7 \times 5000 + 0.3 \times (-2000) = 3500 - 600 = 2900 \text{ (10,000 KRW)}
$$

A positive expected value does not necessarily mean you should heavily push this product. You must also consider whether the company can withstand the scale of the 30% scenario where a loss occurs, because expected value only shows the average outcome — it does not tell you the impact in a worst-case scenario. Initial production volume and the reorder (additional production) budget should be allocated by considering both the expected value and the scale the company can endure in the worst-case scenario.

| Scenario | Probability | Outcome (10,000 KRW) | Probability × Outcome |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Success | 70% | +5,000 | +3,500 |
| Failure | 30% | -2,000 | -600 |
| **Total expected value** | 100% | — | **+2,900** |

## A Practical Calculation

Consider how to divide a limited production budget across several styles. Suppose style A has a high probability of success but a small profit if successful, while style B has a low probability of success but a large profit if successful. Calculating and comparing the expected value of each style lets you judge which one deserves a larger share of the budget.

However, putting the entire budget into one style based on expected value alone is risky, because probabilities are only estimates, and actual outcomes can differ from the estimate. The practical principle is to increase the budget share toward styles with higher expected value, while still spreading production across multiple styles, so that even if one prediction is badly wrong, the whole thing does not collapse.

## Fashion Business Case: 30% Initial, 70% Reactive Production

Many SPA brands do not mass-produce a new product from the start. Instead, they produce only about 30% of the total expected demand as an initial batch, watch actual sell-through speed, and then fill the remaining 70% through reactive production.

This strategy is a practical application of probabilistic thinking. Because the demand for a new product cannot be known precisely before launch, a small initial volume is used to first gain information about market response, and that information is used to reduce uncertainty in the remaining production decision. If all the volume were produced upfront and the forecast turned out wrong, inventory losses would be large; but starting with a small quantity limits the size of failure while still allowing the company to quickly scale up volume and seize the opportunity when the response is good. This is the result of choosing not "the most accurate forecast" but "the least risky way to produce."

## Real-World Case: Zara's Small-Batch Initial Production

Zara is widely known for a strategy of not mass-producing new products from the start, instead producing only a small portion of expected demand first and sending it out to stores. According to case studies including those from Harvard Business School, Zara collects both store sales data and on-the-ground feedback from staff about "products customers looked for but weren't available," and quickly ramps up additional production, with a short lead time of just 2 to 4 weeks, only for styles that get a good response.

Thanks to this approach, Zara is known to keep its markdown (discounted clearance sale) ratio at a level much lower than the industry average (roughly 30-40%). Rather than trying to precisely predict demand from the start, it gains information from a small volume, uses that information to limit the size of failure, and quickly scales up its bet on products that succeed. This is a prime example of the probabilistic thinking covered in this chapter — specifically, the "defensive strategy for being less wrong" — being implemented in an actual supply chain design.

## Chapter Summary

- The goal of probabilistic thinking is not accurate prediction, but avoiding big failure.
- A portfolio strategy that combines basic and trend products lowers the overall probability of failure.
- Expected value is the sum of probability multiplied by outcome, and serves as a criterion for comparing options.
- Even with a high expected value, you must also examine whether the worst-case scenario is a scale the company can endure.
- The strategy of splitting initial production from reactive production is a prime example of applying probabilistic thinking to actual production planning.
