# Chapter 5: Comparing Groups

If you lump all customers and products together into one mass, no strategy can become sharp. The starting point for using data meaningfully is **dividing and comparing along appropriate criteria**. This chapter covers how to divide groups, and how to check afterward whether the difference between them is real.

## The Classification Criterion Is the Competitive Advantage

Given the same customer data, completely different strategies emerge depending on what criterion you use to divide it. Dividing by age reveals generational marketing; dividing by purchase frequency reveals loyal customers versus those at risk of churning; dividing by style preference reveals a curation strategy.

In other words, the classification criterion itself is a competitive advantage. Even if a competitor has the same data, being able to divide customers and products by a more sophisticated, commercially meaningful criterion translates into differentiation. A finely tuned, behavior-based criterion like "customers who prefer minimal tones and only respond to sales" is often more useful for actual marketing and product planning than a demographically lumped segment like "women in their twenties."

## Segments and Clusters

There are broadly two ways to divide groups.

- **Segment**: Dividing by criteria a person decides in advance, such as age, gender, or region. The criteria are clear and easy to interpret, but may not fully capture actual differences in taste.
- **Cluster**: A way of letting the data itself find similar groups by combining multiple variables, such as purchase patterns, click history, and style preference. This can discover taste groups that people had not thought of, but interpreting why a group was formed that way requires additional effort.

In practice, both approaches are used together. Clustering is used to discover new taste groups, and then the characteristics of that group are re-explained in segment language that people can understand.

| | Segment | Cluster |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Who decides the criterion | People | Data (algorithm) |
| Representative criteria | Age, gender, region | Purchase patterns, click history, style preference |
| Advantage | Easy and clear to interpret | Can discover taste groups people missed |
| Disadvantage | May not fully capture actual taste differences | Requires extra effort to interpret why groups formed |

## Fashion A/B Testing

When deciding which of several options is better — such as a product detail page layout or a promotional banner — a more reliable method than intuition or a majority-vote meeting is an **A/B test**. Visitors are randomly split into two groups, one shown option A and the other option B, and their actual responses (click-through rate, conversion rate) are compared.

Random assignment is the key. If the split is not random but based on some specific condition (e.g., time of visit), the result can be distorted by the influence of that condition. Only a random split lets you say that the difference between the two groups comes from the difference between option A and option B themselves.

## What "Statistically Significant" Means

Just because option A's conversion rate came out at 3.2% and option B's at 3.5% doesn't mean you can immediately conclude that option B is better. You need to distinguish whether this difference is a real effect or a random fluctuation.

The **p-value** represents "the probability that a difference of the magnitude observed would occur by chance, assuming there is actually no difference between the two options." If this value is small enough (conventionally below 0.05), it is judged unlikely to be due to chance, and a conclusion is drawn that a real difference exists. This process is called **hypothesis testing**.

If the sample is too small, even random fluctuation alone can produce a large-looking difference, making it hard to trust. Remember that being statistically significant does not mean "the difference is large" — it means **"this difference is likely to be a real, repeatable signal."**

## Fashion Business Case: A Main Banner Curation Experiment

Suppose a commerce platform is deciding which curation to show on its main screen banner: "stylist recommendations" or "bestseller rankings." An A/B test can be designed that randomly exposes both options at the same time and compares click-through rate and subsequent purchase conversion rate.

Here, if you look only at click-through rate, an eye-catching image might have an advantage, so you must also look at the conversion rate that leads all the way to a final purchase. Also, since new visitors and returning customers may respond differently, it is safer to look at results broken down by customer segment as well as the overall average. This is how the techniques of group comparison (segments, A/B testing, significance testing) work together within a single experiment design.

## Real-World Case: ASOS's Product Image and Checkout Experiments

ASOS, a UK-based online fashion retailer, is known to have run repeated A/B tests targeting the arrangement of product images on detail pages and the checkout process. For example, it tested multiple versions of product images to compare which image drew more customer attention and clicks, and it experimentally verified changes in conversion rate when introducing a guest checkout option that allowed payment without signing up for membership.

These experiments follow exactly the A/B testing principle covered in this chapter, in that they verify intuitive judgments — "a prettier-looking image," "a more convenient-looking payment screen" — using actual customer behavior data. Even a decision to change a single small screen can become a judgment backed by evidence rather than intuition, once it goes through a randomized comparison experiment.

## Chapter Summary

- The criterion used to divide data is itself a competitive advantage; the same data can yield completely different strategies depending on the criterion.
- A segment is a clear criterion set by people, while a cluster is a group of similar items the data has found on its own.
- An A/B test is a method of fairly comparing the difference between options through random assignment.
- The p-value and hypothesis testing are tools for judging whether an observed difference is chance or a repeatable signal.
- Statistical significance tells you not how large the difference is, but whether the difference can be trusted.
