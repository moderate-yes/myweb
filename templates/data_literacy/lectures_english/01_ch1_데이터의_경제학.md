# Chapter 1: The Economics of Data

The value of data does not come simply from collecting a large amount of it. Data becomes a true business asset only **when it can be found, connected, corrected, and used for decision-making at low cost, exactly when it is needed.** This chapter goes one step further than defining data, and examines what costs and value arise from holding and using data in a fashion business.

> **Something to think about**
> A store's warehouse has ten years of paper ledgers piled up, alongside digital sales data recorded starting today. Which one contains more information, and which one has higher business value?

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this unit, you will be able to:

- Distinguish between the value of data's existence and the value of its use, and explain the difference.
- Give examples of the connectivity and scalability of digital data, and of the costs of managing data.
- Explain how automation builds the data foundation for insight.
- Identify points in your own fashion work where the cost of repeated errors outweighs the cost of using data.

## Key Terms

- **Connectivity**: The property of linking separated pieces of information — such as products, customers, and orders — using a common reference.
- **Scalability**: The property of being able to keep applying the same rules and structure even as data or users grow.
- **Data debt**: The accumulating cost of continuing to store and manage data that is unused or of low quality.
- **Marginal cost**: The additional cost incurred in storing or using one more unit of data.

## Recording, Modifying, and Deleting Data

Data does not exist only inside computers. The names of unpaid vendors that a market merchant wrote in a notebook, the product names and price cards in a clothing store, and a designer's sketches and fabric swatches can all be considered data in that they carry information. Structured and unstructured information that humans can record and interpret essentially belongs to the domain of data.

The issue is **not whether data exists, but the cost of using it**. Information recorded in a paper ledger is data too, but finding a specific customer's transaction history among thousands of pages of ledgers, or aggregating inventory across multiple stores in real time, requires enormous time and labor. Physical records are difficult to copy, search, modify, and share, and are hard for multiple people to use at the same time.

Digital data, by contrast, has relatively low costs for storage, search, modification, duplication, and transmission. Once well structured, the same data can be reused repeatedly for inventory management, sales analysis, customer recommendations, and demand forecasting. The real value of digitization is not in moving paper onto a screen, but comes from the following two properties.

- **Connectivity**: Different pieces of information — such as products, customers, orders, and inventory — can be linked through identifiers.
- **Scalability**: The same rules can be applied and automated even as the volume of data and the number of users grows.

Therefore, rather than saying analog records are not data, it is more accurate to call them **data with a high cost of use**. The economics of data is not just about what information exists, but about how much it costs to put that information into a state where it can actually be used for decision-making.

## The Gradual Decline in the Cost of Data Tools

In the past, storing and analyzing large-scale data required expensive servers and specialized personnel. Today, with the spread of cloud computing, SaaS, and open-source tools, even small brands can manage sales, inventory, and customer behavior data at relatively low cost.

However, lower storage costs do not mean all data should be collected unconditionally. Collecting data requires setting input rules, correcting errors, protecting personal information, and discarding old data. Beyond storage cost, the following costs continue to arise.

- The cost of generating and entering data
- The cost of cleansing data to unify formats and naming
- The cost of maintaining systems and managing access permissions
- The cost of errors from making wrong decisions based on incorrect data
- Data debt from continuing to store data that is not used

In other words, the price of data cannot be calculated from server fees alone. **We must look at the total cost of keeping data in a trustworthy state.**

## The Economic Properties of Data: Non-Rivalry and Non-Excludability

In economics, goods are classified by two properties. **Rivalry** is the property that when one person uses something, another person cannot use the same thing; **excludability** is the property of being able to prevent someone who has not paid from using it. A single garment is a rival, excludable private good. If one person is wearing it, another person cannot wear it at the same time, and if payment is not made, the store can refuse to sell it.

Data is different.

- **Non-rivalry**: Even if one brand uses yesterday's sales data for analysis, there is no physical constraint preventing another department or partner from using the same data at the same time, because the cost of copying it is close to zero.
- **Excludability is a matter of choice**: Data itself is hard to prevent from being copied, but excludability can be artificially created through access permissions, encryption, and contracts. This is why companies **build fences to impose excludability**, such as database access rights, API keys, and paid reports.

The fact that these two properties are mixed together is what makes data difficult to handle. In terms of cost, it is a good with nearly zero marginal cost (non-rival), yet at the same time companies want to protect it as an exclusive asset (want to make it excludable). As a result, data behaves less like a pure private good or a pure public good, and more like a **club good**. Within a brand, multiple teams can use the same customer data simultaneously and share the cost, while access is blocked to outside competitors to protect competitive advantage.

| | Rival | Non-rival |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Excludable | Private good — clothing, shoes | Club good — internal sales data, paid reports |
| Non-excludable | Common-pool resource — a crowded shared display table | Public good — public statistics, open source |

## Economies of Scale and Network Effects

Data exhibits **economies of scale**, where the cost of processing one additional record falls as more data accumulates. Take a recommendation algorithm as an example: a model trained on the purchase histories of 100 customers is likely to have more error than one trained on the histories of a million customers. Because the infrastructure for storing and cleansing data is already built, the marginal cost of adding more data is much smaller than the initial cost of building that infrastructure.

On top of this, **network effects** often operate together in data businesses — a phenomenon where the value of a service itself grows as the number of users grows.

- As more brands join a fashion platform, consumers gain more options, which makes the platform more attractive.
- As the number of users grows, data such as reviews, styling photos, and click logs accumulate faster.
- As accumulated data improves the quality of recommendations and search, this in turn attracts even more users.

This cycle is called the **data network effect**. When economies of scale combine with network effects, a structure emerges in which the platform that grows first gains a much greater advantage over latecomers. This explains why large e-commerce and fashion platforms are willing to absorb losses early on in order to rapidly acquire users and data.

## Automation First, Insight Second

The ways companies use data can broadly be divided into **business automation** and **insight extraction**. People new to data analysis are often first drawn to insights that reveal hidden patterns and overturn strategy. In practice, however, stabilizing and automating repetitive work with data often comes first.

The reason is that the return on investment is easier to predict. If you invest in analytics infrastructure hoping for insights that will help with new product planning, it is hard to calculate ROI in advance — it is uncertain what insight will be found, whether it will be reflected in decisions, and whether it will actually translate into sales.

Automation, by contrast, allows the effect to be measured relatively clearly. If manual invoice entry is converted to an automated entry system, you can compare the build cost against the savings in working time, labor cost, and entry errors, all in numbers. For an automated ordering system, you can measure changes in stockout rate, excess inventory, and order lead time.

More importantly, automation accumulates consistent data along the way.

> Digitizing repetitive work → stable data quality → accumulation of comparable records → analysis and insight

It is common for analysis toward insight to happen on top of the data foundation built for automation. **Automation is not the opposite of insight — it is the soil in which reliable insight grows.**

## Collecting Data for Data Analysis

Not all data is naturally generated in the course of business operations. Information that does not remain in the system — such as new customer preferences or reasons for not purchasing — must be collected separately. Surveys, interviews, wear tests, and store observation are typical examples.

However, the approach of "let's just collect it now, we might use it later" should be avoided. Data collection should be designed only after first defining the decision-making question.

1. What decision are we trying to make?
2. What do we need to compare in order to make that decision?
3. Does the necessary data already exist?
4. If we collect it newly, what will be the cost and the bias?
5. If the result turns out differently, will actual behavior change too?

For example, if you are trying to decide the color lineup for the next season, a survey question like "What is your favorite color?" alone is not enough, because respondents' stated preferences can differ from their actual purchasing behavior. Connecting survey results to search, click, cart, purchase, and return data allows for a more realistic judgment.

## The Data Market and Price Formation

Data has no fixed price list. The same customer purchase history can have different value depending on who uses it. It may be highly useful to an MD planning new products, but of little significance to a logistics staff member. That is why the price of data is set not by its production cost, but by **the degree of improvement in decision-making that using the data provides**.

The ways data is traded can broadly be divided into three types.

- **Direct sale**: A trend research firm provides seasonal color and material trend reports for a subscription fee.
- **Data licensing**: A fashion platform allows brands to view sales and search data within a limited scope, for a usage fee.
- **Indirect exchange**: Consumers use an app for free in exchange for providing behavioral data to the platform, which the platform then converts into advertising and recommendation revenue. It looks free on the surface, but the data itself is the payment.

What makes the data market different from an ordinary product market is **asymmetric information**. Buyers cannot know the exact quality of data until they actually open it up, and sellers have an incentive to overstate its accuracy. This is why devices such as providing sample reports, return policies, and disclosing verifiable metrics (response rate, collection period, sample size) become key elements in building trust in data transactions.

## Data in the Platform Economy and Two-Sided Markets

A fashion e-commerce platform is a **two-sided market that simultaneously connects two different groups** — consumers and the brands selling on the platform. Platforms often balance this by offering low prices or free benefits to one side while charging fees to the other. For example, consumers may receive free shipping and reward points, while brands on the platform pay sales commissions and advertising fees.

In this structure, data acts as the glue connecting both sides. Consumers' search, click, and purchase data is used to determine the display order of a brand's products and advertising rates, while a brand's sales data is in turn used to refine the recommendations shown to consumers. The better a platform connects the data it accumulates from both sides, the stronger its competitiveness becomes in both markets.

However, this structure also results in data becoming concentrated at the platform. An individual brand can only see part of how its products are consumed within the platform, while the platform can see the full picture across many brands. This **information asymmetry** leads into issues of data ownership and fair competition, which we will cover later.

## Is Using Data Really Necessary in Fashion Business?

Not every fashion brand needs the same level of data systems. A small designer brand where the creator's reputation and scarcity are central may rely heavily on intuition and aesthetic judgment. If the brand has few customers and is centered on made-to-order production, the cost of building a complex forecasting system may exceed the benefit it provides.

Conversely, an SPA or e-commerce business with many SKUs, sales across multiple channels, and short product replacement cycles is difficult to run without data, because inventory by color and size, sell-through rate, return rate, and discount rate all move simultaneously. As scale grows, the cost of grasping the overall situation through individual memory and experience alone increases sharply.

So the question is not "does fashion need data?" but should instead be reframed as:

> **Where in our brand is the cost of repeatedly getting things wrong without data greater than the cost of using data?**

A brand does not need to hand every judgment over to data. Repeatable, measurable areas can be handled by data and automation, while areas with no single right answer — such as new aesthetics and brand direction — can be handled by human sense and experimentation.

## Data Ownership, Privacy, and Policy Costs

As the economic value of data grows, so does the question of "whose data is this?" A customer's purchase history and size information are both a brand's asset and personal information about that individual customer. Because of this duality, using data comes with **policy and legal costs** in addition to storage and cleansing costs.

- **Personal data protection regulation**: Korea's Personal Information Protection Act and Europe's GDPR require that collection purposes be made clear, that use beyond that purpose be restricted, and that customers' right to request deletion of their data be guaranteed.
- **Data portability and consent management**: If customers must be able to withdraw consent or move their data to another service at any time, companies must have systems in place in advance to handle this.
- **The cost of security incidents**: A leak of customer data creates both direct and indirect costs at once — fines and a loss of trust.

These regulations should not be seen merely as constraints, but as part of the economics of data. A strategy of collecting as much data as possible without factoring in the cost of compliance may look cheap in the short run, but can come back as a much larger legal and reputational cost in the long run. Conversely, if consent procedures and access rights management are built into data design from the start, the cost of regulatory compliance becomes a predictable fixed cost. **This is why "whose consent will we obtain, and how will we use it" must be included in the cost-benefit calculation just as much as "how much will we collect."**

## Fashion Business Case: The Demand Signal Left in the Fitting Room

If a customer takes a product into the fitting room but does not purchase it, no sales data remains in the POS system. However, this behavior may be a signal that the customer was interested in the product, but gave up on purchasing due to fit, price, material, or how it worked as an outfit.

If we can connect fitting attempts to purchase outcomes, we can ask questions that sales figures alone cannot reveal.

- Which products are tried on often but rarely purchased?
- Is the conversion rate low for certain sizes?
- Are there many online views but a drop-off after in-store trying-on?
- Is it a design problem, or a fit or price problem?

Behavioral data that looks trivial like this can be used for product improvement and inventory decisions once connected with other data. However, we must first examine whether the cost of collecting and operating it exceeds the expected value.

## Real-World Case: Zalando's Fashion Data Asset

> Before publishing, the year of the research announcement, the SKU scale, and the scope of use in the case below must be verified against Zalando's official research materials or the original paper, and a source link should be added.

Zalando, Europe's largest online fashion platform, stated in research published in 2016 that between 2011 and 2016 it integrated the image, attribute, and sales data of approximately 1.33 million SKUs sold on its platform into a single data asset called "Fashion DNA." Each individual product's data may not seem to hold much value on its own, but once a large dataset accumulated over several years is integrated, it becomes a reusable asset that can simultaneously power several different services on top of it, such as recommendations, search, and new product sales forecasting.

This case illustrates well the economies of scale covered in this chapter. Cleansing and structuring data once involves a cost, but the asset created that way could then be reused repeatedly — at nearly zero marginal cost — for multiple purposes such as recommendation systems, inventory forecasting, and new product catalog mapping.

## Chapter Summary

- Analog information is also data, but it has high costs for search, connection, and scaling.
- The value of digital data comes from low processing cost, connectivity, and scalability.
- Even as storage costs fall, cleansing, management, error, and security costs do not disappear.
- Data is non-rival, but companies turn it into an asset by creating excludability through access rights and contracts.
- Data operates under overlapping economies of scale and network effects, creating a structure that favors leading companies.
- The price of data is set not by production cost but by the degree of decision-making improvement it provides when used, and asymmetric information creates trust issues in transactions.
- Platforms use data as the glue connecting consumers and brands in a two-sided market, and in the process information becomes concentrated at the platform.
- Automation creates measurable efficiency and accumulates reliable data.
- Data collection should start from a clear decision-making question.
- Using data involves not just storage costs but also policy costs such as personal data protection and consent management.
- The need for data in fashion business varies depending on brand scale and operational complexity.
- The best division of labor is for machines to handle repetition and for people to handle direction and experimentation.
