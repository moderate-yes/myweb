# Chapter 6: Proving Relationships

Just because two phenomena move together does not mean you can conclude that one causes the other. One of the most common mistakes made by people working with data is confusing **a phenomenon that moves together (correlation)** with **a phenomenon that actually causes another (causation)**. This chapter covers how to distinguish between the two.

## Variables That Affect Sales

If a particular product's sales suddenly increase, there are many candidate causes. The weather may have turned colder, an influencer may have posted a photo wearing it, or a discount may have started. Because these candidates can all exist at the same point in time, it is not easy to determine which one is the actual cause just by looking at the data.

The problem is that when multiple variables change at the same time, it is easy to hastily point to a cause based only on the relationship that appears on the surface. In reality, all three factors might have worked together, or only one might be the true cause while the rest coincidentally overlapped.

## Correlation and Causation

**Correlation** refers to a statistical pattern in which two variables move together, while **causation** refers to a relationship in which a change in one variable actually brings about a change in another. A famous example: "sandal sales and ice cream sales increase together" is a correlation, but sandals do not cause ice cream sales. Both phenomena stem from a common cause — **hot weather**.

Behind a correlation like this, there may be a hidden, invisible **third variable (a confounding variable)**. In fashion data too, just because "sales of a particular color product and its social media mentions increased together" does not mean you can conclude that the social media mentions caused the sales. There may be a common factor influencing both, such as a seasonal trend or a celebrity wearing the item.

## The Ladder of Causal Inference in Fashion

The process of confirming causation can be compared to climbing a ladder step by step.

1. **Observed correlation**: Look at the data and discover a pattern that moves together.
2. **Confirming causation through experiment**: Keep all other conditions the same and change only the variable suspected of being the cause. For example, expose the same product to two groups but apply a discount to only one, then compare the difference in sales.
3. **Forecasting the next season**: Once causation is confirmed, use it as a basis to forecast what's ahead and reflect it in decision-making.

If you stop at observation alone, there is a risk of mistaking a coincidental pattern for a cause; going through an experiment lets you reach a much more trustworthy conclusion. However, not every question can be tested experimentally, so when an experiment is difficult, statistical analysis that controls for multiple variables is used to get as close to causation as possible.

| Step | What it does | Reliability |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Observed correlation | Discover a pattern moving together in the data | Low (could be coincidence) |
| 2. Confirming causation through experiment | Change only the causal variable and compare outcomes | High |
| 3. Forecasting the next season | Apply the confirmed causation to decision-making | Used as a basis for action |

## Proving Relationships and Fashion AI

Fit prediction and recommendation engines are, in the end, systems that learn the relationships among customers, products, and behavior. However, most AI models only **learn correlational patterns** and cannot on their own judge whether that relationship is actually causal. A recommendation like "this customer will like this product" is a prediction based on past correlation, not a causal explanation of why they like that product.

That is why, when using AI-generated predictions for decision-making, people need to also review whether the prediction is based on a stable causal relationship or is merely a coincidentally repeated pattern.

## Fashion Business Case: Short Padded Jacket Sales — Design or Cold Snap?

If a particular brand's short padded jacket sales grew significantly year-over-year, the brand might want to interpret this as the result of design improvement. But if sales of short padded jackets and outerwear in general from other brands also grew over the same period, it's possible that the common cause was that winter was unusually cold that year, rather than design.

To tell these apart, you must look beyond your own products and compare against the category overall, competitor trends, and temperature data. If your product's sales growth rate is notably higher than competitors', design is more likely the factor; if the entire industry grew similarly, external factors like weather are more likely to have had a large influence. Distinguishing the cause correctly is what allows the right lesson to be reflected in planning for the next season.

## Real-World Case: Uniqlo and the Japan Weather Association's Weather Data

Fast Retailing, which operates Uniqlo, is known to use a demand forecasting service from the Japan Weather Association that combines weather data with sales data. The Japan Weather Association provides a service that connects past weather data, forecasts up to two weeks ahead, and even long-term climate outlooks with product sales data, to analyze under which temperature and precipitation conditions demand for which categories rises and falls.

This case connects directly with the distinction between correlation and causation covered in this chapter. Rather than simply observing the correlation that "outerwear sold well on days when the temperature dropped," it statistically verifies patterns that repeat across regions and time periods, finding the conditions under which weather actually becomes a cause of demand change. This confirmed causal relationship is actually used to adjust the timing of promotions and order quantities.

## Chapter Summary

- A phenomenon that moves together (correlation) does not necessarily mean cause and effect (causation).
- Behind a correlation, there may be a hidden third variable (confounding variable) that influenced both phenomena at once.
- Causation becomes more trustworthy when it starts from an observed correlation and goes through experimental verification.
- Most recommendation and prediction AI models learn correlational patterns, and the causal validity of their results must be reviewed by people.
- To accurately distinguish the cause of a sales change, you must compare not just your own data but the category overall and external factors as well.
