# Chapter 8: Online Fashion Content and Vanity Metrics

Content with a high view count does not necessarily generate revenue. Numbers that look good and numbers that actually move the business can be different things. This chapter covers how to distinguish flashy-looking but decision-unhelpful **vanity metrics** from metrics that reflect real behavior and value.

## Digital Content That Gets Ignored

When a carefully produced lookbook or brand film doesn't get the view count expected, many teams plan the next piece of content based on subjective judgments like "make it more emotional" or "make it trendier." But feeling alone makes it hard to know exactly what the problem was.

To find the cause, you need to look at **behavioral data**, not impressions. Looking at how much people scrolled through the content, at which scene they dropped off, and how often they moved from the content to a product page can reveal much more specific causes, such as "the load time was slow" or "it didn't have the information people wanted," rather than just "it wasn't pretty enough."

## Measurement and Improvement

Without a measurable goal, there's no way to compare whether content that's been improved has actually gotten better. Repeating the resolution "let's make it better next time" just leads to repeating the same mistakes.

On the other hand, setting a measurable goal like "increase scroll completion rate by 10 percentage points" or "double the click-through rate from content to product detail" lets you check, after revising the content, whether that change actually moved you closer to the goal. Only with this kind of repeated measure-and-improve structure does content production shift from repetition based on intuition to data-driven experimentation.

## Vanity Metrics

A **vanity metric** refers to a metric that looks like performance on the surface but doesn't directly explain actual business outcomes. View counts, likes, and follower counts are typical examples. These metrics are a signal that attention was received, but whether that attention translates into actual purchases or brand loyalty must be checked separately.

The reason vanity metrics are dangerous is that they're easy to inflate, but there's no guarantee that revenue grows along with them. A sensational thumbnail meant to boost view count could instead attract visitors who don't match the brand image, lowering the conversion rate. When the metric itself becomes the goal, you can end up with a situation where the numbers go up but the business doesn't actually improve.

## Behavioral and Value Metrics

The alternative to vanity metrics is metrics closer to actual purchasing behavior and long-term value.

- **Cart conversion rate**: The share of people who viewed content or a detail page and actually added the item to their cart.
- **Size chart click-through rate**: A signal of serious purchase consideration; you should also check whether it leads to an actual purchase after the click.
- **Retention**: The rate at which a customer who has purchased once returns or repurchases.
- **LTV (Customer Lifetime Value)**: The cumulative value a single customer brings to the brand over the course of the relationship.

These metrics are more cumbersome to measure and less immediately visible than view counts, but they reflect actual sales and the brand's long-term health far more accurately. A good content strategy does not ignore vanity metrics entirely, but rather uses them as a reference signal of initial interest, while making the final judgment based on behavioral and value metrics.

| | Vanity metrics | Behavioral/value metrics |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Examples | View count, likes, follower count | Cart conversion rate, size chart click-through rate, retention, LTV |
| Difficulty of measurement | Easy, immediate | Relatively cumbersome |
| Connection to purchase outcomes | Indirect, uncertain | Direct |
| How to use | Reference as an early interest signal | Basis for final decision-making |

## Fashion Business Case: Comparing Model Shots and Review Photos

Suppose a brand places officially shot model photos and customer-submitted wear-review photos together on a detail page. Comparing view counts alone between the two pieces of content might show little difference, but comparing scroll depth and dwell time could reveal a different picture.

If users skim through model shots quickly but linger longer and flip through several review photos, this could be a signal that customers trust seeing an actual fit on a body and in a situation similar to their own. If this observation holds, it could lead to a strategy of moving review content higher up on the detail page, or more actively collecting review photos. This is an insight that would not have been discovered by looking only at the vanity metric of view count.

## Real-World Case: ASOS's Marketing Metric Rebalancing

ASOS is known to have once concentrated around 80% of its total marketing budget on performance marketing, but ended up experiencing the opposite of what was intended — new customer acquisition slowed and the cost of acquiring each customer rose. By focusing on maximizing immediately visible metrics like click-through rate and impressions, it relatively neglected the factors that lead to long-term brand trust and repeat visits.

ASOS later stated that it shifted direction toward a "balanced" marketing strategy that also increased investment related to brand awareness. This case demonstrates, at the concrete decision-making level of a marketing budget, this chapter's core message that short-term metrics like view count or clicks do not necessarily translate into actual growth.

## Chapter Summary

- When content performance is low, look for the cause in behavioral data such as scrolling, drop-off, and clicks rather than in impressions.
- A measurable goal is needed for content improvement to become data-driven experimentation rather than intuition.
- Vanity metrics like view count and likes are only signals of interest and don't directly explain purchase outcomes.
- Behavioral and value metrics like cart conversion rate, retention, and LTV are closer to actual business outcomes.
- Vanity metrics should be referenced as an early signal, but the final judgment should be made using metrics that reflect behavior and value.
