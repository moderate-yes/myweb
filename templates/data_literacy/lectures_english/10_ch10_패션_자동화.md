# Chapter 10: Fashion Automation and the Experimental Environment

Automation is powerful for stabilizing repetitive work, but it cannot replace the creative experimentation that is at the core of fashion business. This chapter covers what to automate, what to leave to human experimentation, and how to design that boundary.

## A Game You Always Start Out Winning

The task best suited to automation is **work whose outcome is already predictable and repetitive**. A system that automatically places an order when the inventory of a constantly-selling basic product falls below a certain level is a typical example. Because demand for basic products is stable, the probability of failure is low as long as the rules are set well.

This kind of work can be called "a game you always start out winning," because it is not a new challenge with an uncertain outcome, but the repetition of an already-verified pattern. Automating this area frees up human time and attention for areas that require far more uncertain and creative judgment.

## A Culture That Allows Experimentation

There is something essential that must come before automation: **an environment where small failures can be learned from quickly**. New designs or plans cannot be known to be correct from the start, so a cycle of trying small, seeing the result, and improving the next attempt is necessary.

In this cycle, failure is a natural cost of learning. In an organizational culture that fears failure excessively, new attempts themselves decrease, and in the end, patterns verified enough to be worth automating never get created. Automation is built on top of results that have already been sufficiently experimented with and verified — it is not something that can be created by skipping experimentation altogether.

## The Risk of Bad Automation

Automation itself is a neutral tool, but **automating unverified judgment actually increases risk**. For example, suppose a rule is created to keep automatically producing more of a product that had a good initial response — but if that initial response was due to a temporary fad or a promotion effect, the automated system cannot tell the difference and keeps increasing production.

The result is mass production of dead stock. A decision that a human would have noticed as an abnormal signal and stopped midway is instead continuously executed by the automated system according to its fixed rules, which can cause the problem to spread faster and larger. It's important to remember that automation is a tool that scales good judgment quickly, but it's equally a tool that scales bad judgment just as quickly.

## The Boundary Between Humans and Machines

The core principle of this chapter is division of labor.

- **What machines are good at**: Repetitive calculations, replenishing inventory according to fixed rules, quickly processing large volumes of data.
- **What humans are good at**: Insight to discover new aesthetics, experimentation in situations with no single right answer, judgment to detect abnormal signals and reconsider the rules themselves.

Dividing roles so that designers and MDs handle insight and experimentation while machines handle repetitive calculation and inventory replenishment lets each side leverage its own strengths. What matters is not treating this boundary as fixed — instead, judgments that have been repeatedly verified are gradually handed over to machines, while people are always moved on to the next layer of uncertain territory.

| | What machines are good at | What humans are good at |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Characteristics | Repetitive, predictable, high-volume processing | No single right answer, detecting abnormal signals, creative judgment |
| Examples | Automated ordering, inventory replenishment, data aggregation | New designs, aesthetic judgment, reconsidering rules |

## Fashion Business Case: Collaboration Between the Data Team and the Design Team

Some tech-driven fashion companies do not fully separate the data team from the design team, but instead build a structure of ongoing collaboration. The data team organizes metrics like sell-through speed, inventory turnover, and return rate and passes them on to the design team, which references these metrics but still makes final design decisions independently.

In this collaboration, the data team takes on the role of "automating already-verified patterns and quickly flagging abnormal signals," while the design team takes on the role of "experimenting with new attempts that data cannot explain." A structure where the two teams share information without excessively intervening in each other's decisions is what an organization where automation and creative experimentation coexist looks like.

## Real-World Case: Inditex's Automated Logistics Hub

Inditex, Zara's parent company, has built a large-scale automated logistics center in Zaragoza, Spain, expanding a system that handles online and pickup orders in large volumes through automated sorting robots and automated storage facilities. It has also invested in logistics robotics startups to automate tasks that existing robot technology finds difficult to handle, pursuing a strategy of progressively handing repetitive, predictable logistics work over to machines.

At the same time, the judgment at the core of Inditex's competitiveness — "what design should we release this season?" — still remains the responsibility of designers and the planning organization. This is a case where the principle covered in this chapter — automating repeatable logistics and inventory processing while leaving creative judgment to people — can actually be observed at large-scale organizational scale.

## Chapter Summary

- Automation is best suited to work whose outcome is predictable and repetitive.
- Before automation, a culture of experimentation that allows learning quickly from small failures is needed first.
- Automating unverified judgment risks having problems spread faster and larger.
- A good division of labor has machines handle repetitive calculation and rule execution, while humans handle insight, experimentation, and detecting abnormal signals.
- A collaborative structure where the data team and design team share information but respect each other's decision-making territory is effective.
