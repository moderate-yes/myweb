# Chapter 11: AI Optimization and the Intuition of Calculus

When we say an AI "learns," what's actually happening is that it repeats a computational process of gradually reducing error. Rather than diving deep into complex formulas, this chapter focuses on building an intuitive understanding of how AI learns design and demand by reducing error.

## The Fashion Optimization Problem

Fashion production is fundamentally an **optimization problem**. Within limited fabric and budget, you must find the production point that simultaneously minimizes stockouts (too little) and excess inventory (too much). Increasing production lowers the risk of stockouts but raises the risk of excess inventory, and decreasing production raises the opposite risk.

When these two kinds of loss move in opposite directions like this, there exists some point in between where **total loss is smallest**. AI learning has exactly the same structure. A prediction that's too high or too low both increase error, and the goal of learning is to find the point where error is smallest.

## Why We Need Derivatives

The problem is that you can't know "the point where error is smallest" all at once. Instead, the computer moves by checking, bit by bit, in which direction the error decreases from its current position. This process is called **gradient descent**, and can be compared to finding the lowest valley in a mountain while blindfolded — feeling the slope underfoot and moving one step at a time in the downhill direction.

The tool that calculates this "slope" is precisely the **derivative**. The simplest linear function can be expressed as follows.

$$
f(x) = ax + b
$$

Here, $a$ is the slope corresponding to the change in input $x$, and $b$ is the intercept where the graph starts. A derivative calculates how steeply, and in which direction, this function changes at a particular point. In AI learning, the slope of the function representing prediction error is calculated, and parameters like $a$ and $b$ are adjusted little by little in the direction that reduces error.

$$
a_{\text{new}} = a_{\text{old}} - \eta \cdot \frac{\partial \text{error}}{\partial a}
$$

This equation looks complicated, but its meaning is simple: it moves the parameter in the opposite direction to what the slope points (the $-$ sign), by a small step sized according to the learning rate $\eta$ (eta). If the step is too large, it overshoots the valley; if too small, learning becomes excessively slow.

| Iteration | Current $a$ value | Error | Slope direction | Next $a$ value |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 0.10 | Large | Positive (+) | 0.35 |
| 2 | 0.35 | Medium | Positive (+) | 0.48 |
| 3 | 0.48 | Small | Nearly 0 | 0.50 |

## A Small Neural Network Exercise

As a simple example, consider a small model that takes two input values — fabric unit cost and design complexity — and calculates expected sales. At first, the model starts with random values (weights), so its predictions differ greatly from actual sales. The model looks at past data one piece at a time, calculates the difference (error) between prediction and actual, and adjusts the weights little by little in the direction that reduces that error.

Repeating this process hundreds or thousands of times, the model finds, on its own, a pattern for "how to adjust sales predictions as fabric cost rises and design gets more complex." The basic principle of a neural network is learning a pattern purely through repeated error reduction, without a person having to input rules one by one.

## Forward Propagation and Backpropagation

Learning consists of computation in two directions.

- **Forward propagation**: The process of feeding input values (fabric unit cost, design complexity) into the model to produce a prediction (expected sales).
- **Backpropagation**: The process of checking the error between the prediction and the actual value, then tracing back the cause of that error to calculate how much each weight should be adjusted.

Forward propagation produces the prediction, and backpropagation sends the degree of error back, distributed according to each parameter's share of responsibility. Repeating these two processes is the entire process that AI calls "learning."

| | Forward propagation | Backpropagation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Direction | Input → prediction | Error → each parameter |
| What it does | Calculates the prediction | Divides the error's responsibility to compute adjustment values |
| Analogy | Making a draft | Revising based on feedback |

## Learning Point: Connecting to Design Feedback

An AI's error-correction loop is essentially similar to the process a designer goes through revising a sample. A designer makes a draft (forward propagation), checks the actual fit result or feedback (checking the error), and reflects that feedback to slightly revise the next sample (the adjustment corresponding to backpropagation). Repeating this cycle several times, the designer gets progressively closer to a better result.

Understanding AI optimization this way shows that even if the complex formulas feel unfamiliar, the idea underneath them — **a learning method that repeatedly reduces small errors** — is something already familiar and used in design and planning practice.

## Real-World Case: H&M's AI Demand Forecasting

H&M is known to have introduced a machine learning-based demand forecasting model that learns from more than a decade of accumulated sales history combined with real-time signals from social networks. This model detects early patterns of rising interest in a particular color or style and reflects them in order quantity decisions, continuously feeding the error between prediction and actual sales back into learning to improve accuracy.

According to reports, after adopting this system, H&M achieved a significant reduction in excess inventory and raised its sell-through rate, reducing markdowns (inventory discount sales). This is a case where the core idea of gradient descent covered in this chapter — a learning process that repeatedly reduces the error between prediction and actual — led to the concrete outcome of reduced inventory cost in an actual demand forecasting system.

## Chapter Summary

- Fashion production is an optimization problem of finding the optimal point between two losses: stockouts and excess inventory.
- Gradient descent is a method of following the slope at the current point and moving one step at a time in the direction that reduces error.
- A derivative is a tool that calculates in which direction, and how steeply, a function changes at a particular point.
- A neural network learns patterns by repeatedly reducing error and adjusting its weights on its own.
- Forward propagation is the process of producing a prediction, and backpropagation is the process of tracing back the cause of error to adjust parameters.
- An AI's error-correction loop is essentially similar to the process of a designer revising a sample based on feedback.
