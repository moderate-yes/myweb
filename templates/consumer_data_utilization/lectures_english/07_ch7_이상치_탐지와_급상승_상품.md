# Chapter 7: Detecting Trending Products With Anomaly Detection

Catching, as quickly as possible, a product whose sales suddenly rise or fall regardless of its usual trend is called **anomaly detection**. This chapter uses **six sigma**, a statistical technique also used to catch defective products, to find "products that are suddenly selling well right now" using nothing but sales data.

## Key Terms

- **Anomaly detection**: Analysis that finds objects deviating from a data's general trend. Searching in English for "anomaly detection" turns up more material than the literal Korean translation does.
- **Normal distribution**: A bell-shaped probability distribution where values cluster near the mean and become rarer toward either extreme.
- **Probability density function**: A function that expresses how likely a value is to occur as the height of a graph.
- **Six sigma**: A method that divides a normal distribution into several standard-deviation bands (sigmas) and flags a value as an anomaly once it falls outside a defined band.

## Why Anomaly Detection Matters

Anomaly detection isn't one specific algorithm — it's an umbrella term for an entire approach aimed at solving a purpose. Catching defective products with a smart-factory camera, or detecting a malfunction from an unusual sound picked up by a sensor mounted on a vehicle, both count as anomaly detection. In an online store, it's used to find products whose sales or click volume are moving differently from usual. Countless methods exist for finding anomalies; this chapter covers the relatively approachable six-sigma method.

## The Logic of Six-Sigma Analysis

The core idea is simple. If a product sold at a similar level — 5, 6, 4 units a day — for eight straight days, then suddenly sold 11 units on day nine, that's a value outside its usual pattern.

1. For each product, compute the mean and standard deviation of its sales over the past several days. Say a product has a mean of 25 and a standard deviation of 1.
2. Assume this product's sales follow a **normal distribution** defined by that mean and standard deviation. Values close to the mean have high probability density (a tall point on the graph); values farther from the mean have low density.
3. Plug today's sales value into this distribution to compute its probability density. A value near the usual 25 produces high density; an unusual value like 32 produces low density.
4. A low-density value above the mean is classified as a **spike**; a low-density value below the mean is classified as a **drop**.

Dividing the graph into six (or three) bands by standard deviation and checking whether a value falls past the last band is where the name "six sigma" comes from.

## The Analysis Process: From Preprocessing to Verdict

Real sales data accumulates by transaction timestamp, like this:

| Timestamp | Product | Quantity |
|---|---|---|
| 2023-01-02 19:35 | product_0 | 1 |
| 2023-01-02 14:20 | product_21 | 2 |
| 2023-01-01 14:55 | product_0 | 1 |

Before anomaly detection can run, this needs to become a pivot table of product × period (typically a day), listing each product's daily sales in a single row. Daily is the default period, but a store with very high sales volume might aggregate by half-day or even by the hour instead.

```python
import pandas as pd
from scipy.stats import norm

# Assume `daily` is a pivot table: rows are products, columns are dates,
# and values are that product's sales on that date.
mean = daily.mean(axis=1)
std = daily.std(axis=1)

today = daily.iloc[:, -1]                       # sales on the most recent date
density = norm.pdf(today, loc=mean, scale=std)   # today's probability density

threshold = density.quantile(0.05)  # example: treat the bottom 5% as "rare"
is_unusual = density < threshold
is_spike = is_unusual & (today > mean)
is_drop = is_unusual & (today < mean)
```

This code translates the concept from the original course into a pandas/scipy example. `norm.pdf` computes the probability density of a specific value under an assumed normal distribution — a direct, literal expression in code of the principle that a value's density shrinks the further it sits from the mean.

## Connecting Results to Action

Finding an anomaly by itself accomplishes nothing. A product classified as a spike can be surfaced on the store's homepage or moved to the top of the bestseller page, an outfit photo featuring it can go up on the brand's social media quickly, or exposure can be boosted through portal keyword and banner ads. A product classified as a drop, conversely, can be investigated for a root cause, or discounted to clear inventory. Since it's unrealistic for a person to eyeball a graph for 100 or 200 products every day, this kind of judgment is usually automated — shared with merchandisers and marketers by email or messenger each morning, or wired into a store's API so display placement updates automatically.

The same logic doesn't have to apply only to products. Reframing the unit as a customer or a region — a customer suddenly buying much more, a region suddenly buying much less — lets the same six-sigma logic drive marketing strategy tailored to customer behavior or regional characteristics.

## Chapter Summary

- Anomaly detection isn't a single algorithm — it's an umbrella term for the whole approach of finding what deviates from the norm as fast as possible.
- The six-sigma method assumes a normal distribution from a product's mean and standard deviation of sales, then classifies today's value as a spike or drop based on whether its probability density is low and it sits above or below the mean.
- Transaction-level data has to be reshaped into a product × period pivot table before anomaly detection can run.
- Detection results only matter once they connect to concrete actions — adjusting display placement, posting to social media, running ads — and an automated system for sharing them.
- The same logic can be reapplied at the level of a customer or a region instead of a product.
