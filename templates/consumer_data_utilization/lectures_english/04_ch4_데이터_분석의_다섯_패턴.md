# Chapter 4: Five Patterns of Data Analysis

"Data analysis" sounds broad and vague, but the patterns that show up again and again in practice are fewer than you'd expect. Characterization, discrimination, frequent patterns, classification and regression, clustering, and anomaly detection. Knowing these five branches makes it much faster to judge "what kind of analysis does this problem call for." The patterns introduced in this chapter aren't the only ones that exist, but they are the backbone practitioners run into most often in the apparel industry.

> **Something to think about**
> "What are the traits of products that sell well in our store?" and "Will this customer buy from our store again next month?" call for different kinds of analysis. Think first about how the two questions differ in character.

## Key Terms

- **Target class**: The object of observation or prediction in an analysis. Also called the dependent variable or label.
- **Characterization**: Analysis that summarizes the general traits of a target class.
- **Frequent pattern**: Analysis that finds combinations that appear together often, or in a repeated sequence, in the data.
- **Clustering**: Analysis that groups data by similarity without a predefined answer.

## Types of Classes: Categorical and Continuous

Before explaining the patterns, we need to define **class**. A class is the object of observation in an analysis. If the goal is "find products that sell well on rainy days," the object of observation (the class) is the product, and weather and sales volume are the conditions.

Classes broadly split into two kinds: **categorical** classes, such as gender or age group, where the boundaries between values are clear, and **continuous** classes, such as price or temperature, where the boundaries are not. This distinction carries directly into the difference between classification (predicting a categorical value) and regression (predicting a continuous value), covered below. (There are also time-series classes, like stock prices or sentences, where an earlier event affects a later one, but this chapter doesn't cover those.)

## Characterization and Discrimination

**Characterization** summarizes the general traits of a target class. Finding the price range where the most units sold last summer is a typical example, and the core tool is **measuring central tendency** — seeing which value shows up most. Charting how sales are distributed across categories to see how spread out the data is — **measuring dispersion** — also counts as characterization. Pie charts showing product mix and bar charts showing sales change over time are common outputs of characterization.

**Discrimination** compares a target class's traits against one or more other classes. A pivot table comparing category sales across seasons is a typical example.

| | Season 1 | Season 2 |
|---|---|---|
| T-shirts | 43 | 51 |
| Jeans | 29 | 35 |

Comparing a competitor against your own store is also a form of discrimination. Characterization and discrimination look simple enough to invite the reaction "I could do that myself" — but their strength lies in convincing a room full of people with a single, intuitive table. In practice, the preprocessing needed to produce that simple table is often harder than the analysis itself.

## Frequent Patterns

**Frequent pattern** analysis finds **co-occurrence** — items that frequently appear together — or **sequential occurrence** — items that appear in a repeated order over time — within a dataset. A white T-shirt tending to be bought alongside jeans is an example of co-occurrence; buying a matching bag a few days after buying a card wallet is an example of sequential occurrence. Reshaping the data slightly can also surface patterns linking demographics to products, such as "male customers in their twenties buy black socks." The same logic is used in text analysis to find sub-topics that frequently co-occur with a given topic, and machine translation itself can be viewed as a result built on sequential-occurrence algorithms.

## Classification and Regression

**Classification** builds a model that automatically sorts a categorical target class. If a store has too many existing customers to manually identify VIPs each time, building a model that classifies customers as VIP or non-VIP based on demographics and purchase patterns, then applying it periodically, is an example of automating a class. Conversely, splitting customers into VIP and non-VIP by spending level and then explaining the demographic differences between the two groups with a model such as a decision tree falls under explaining a class concept.

**Regression** applies when the target class is continuous, used to predict a continuous value such as price or temperature, or to understand the relationship between two variables. Identifying the relationship between income level and purchase volume is a typical example. In practice, teams often eyeball the relationship between two variables on a graph first; formally confirming the relationship through regression analysis is more common in academic papers that require hypothesis testing.

## Clustering

**Clustering** splits collected data into multiple groups, used when the target class isn't clearly defined, either to create a new class or to reference one. The resulting subgroups are called **clusters** because they group similar objects together; the method is also called **unsupervised learning**, since there is no predefined answer, or **segmentation**, since it partitions the data. A common use is dividing a large customer base into groups with similar traits for management purposes, and it's also gaining attention as a way to speed up the labeling work AI training requires. It's likewise used to find similar expressions in text or group similar styles in images.

## Anomaly Detection

**Anomaly detection** finds objects that deviate from the data's general trend or model. It's commonly used to catch defective products, and in fashion, to catch products whose sales suddenly spike well beyond the average — trending products. It's also used as a preprocessing step that filters out rarely occurring data points before a more refined analysis. This pattern is the core tool behind both the Python practicum in the next chapter and the "detecting trending products" chapter that follows it.

## Chapter Summary

- The five branches that come up repeatedly in data analysis are characterization/discrimination, frequent patterns, classification/regression, clustering, and anomaly detection.
- Whether the target class is categorical or continuous determines whether you reach for classification or regression.
- Characterization and discrimination look simple but carry real persuasive power, and the preprocessing behind them is often the hard part.
- Clustering is unsupervised learning that groups similar objects without a predefined answer; anomaly detection finds objects that deviate from the norm.
