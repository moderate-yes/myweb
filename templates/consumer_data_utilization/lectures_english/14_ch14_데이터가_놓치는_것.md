# Chapter 14: What Data Misses: Balancing Intuition and Data

Across thirteen units, we've looked at what data can do. This final unit reverses direction: what does data miss, and how should we use data and intuition together? As we close out this course, let's step back and think about the attitude with which all the techniques we've covered should actually be handled.

> **Something to think about**
> Data always records "what already happened." Can data predict a genuinely new design — one nobody has ever bought, because it didn't exist yet?

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this unit, you will be able to:

- Explain what cherry-picking is, and why it's dangerous in data analysis.
- Explain the inherent "backward-looking" limitation of data.
- Give examples from fashion history where intuition led ahead of data.
- Explain, in your own words, why an attitude that combines data with intuition matters.

## Key Terms

- **Cherry-picking**: A biased way of using data that presents only the portion of the full dataset favorable to one's own argument.
- **Backward-looking limitation**: The fundamental limitation, rooted in the fact that data is a record of what already happened, that makes it difficult to capture changes that haven't happened yet.

## Cherry-Picking: Numbers Don't Lie, But People Can Choose Which Numbers to Show

Data itself is objective, as long as it isn't fabricated. But **cherry-picking** — showing only the portion of a large dataset that favors your own argument — is an extremely common trap in data analysis. For example, showing that "this promotion increased sales by 30%" might be true, but if the comparison period happens to be the lowest-sales period on record, that 30% figure distorts the overall picture even though it's technically accurate. Every technique covered in this course — RFM analysis, A/B testing, anomaly detection — can produce entirely different conclusions depending on which baseline period or comparison group is chosen, and that's something to stay vigilant about at all times.

## The Fundamental Limitation of Data: Its Backward-Looking Nature

No matter how sophisticated data analysis becomes, there's a wall it struggles to cross: data is, by nature, **a record of what has already happened**. Data can tell you precisely which products sold well last month or which colors were popular last year, but a new taste or trend that "nobody has said they wanted yet" simply doesn't exist within past data. Wedel and Kannan's well-known 2016 paper in the academic literature, "Marketing Analytics for Data-Rich Environments," broadly addresses this point — that data-driven analysis is a powerful tool for marketing decisions, while at the same time carrying this exact structural limitation. This is precisely why data analysts and marketers need to collaborate closely. Data can tell you precisely "what worked," but "what to try for the first time" still belongs to human intuition and imagination.

## Moments When Intuition Led Data: Intuitive Innovation in Fashion History

Fashion history has moments when designers, working with overwhelming intuition in the absence of any data, created entirely new movements.

- **Vivienne Westwood**: Elevated punk aesthetics into high fashion, establishing as a fashion genre a radical aesthetic that no consumer data of the time could have predicted.
- **Issey Miyake**: Independently researched pleating techniques, producing a material innovation that combined functionality and artistry at once. This wasn't the result of market research, but of long intuitive exploration into the relationship between material and the body.
- **Virgil Abloh**: Dissolved the boundary between streetwear and high fashion, presenting the market with a new consumer base and aesthetic sensibility that existing luxury-industry data had never accounted for.

What these designers share is that data followed afterward to "explain" their success — data never "predicted" their success in advance.

> **Real-world case**
> Jeon Hee-jae and Kim Geon-dong's (2022) research examines how virtual fitting technology in metaverse spaces can expand the fashion consumption experience. Virtual fitting is itself a technology that actively leverages data — body-shape and preference data — but the initial judgment that "consumers will want this new kind of experience" started not from existing consumption data, but from an intuitive forecast about technology and the market. That makes it a good example of how data and intuition, rather than opposing each other, engage in a staged, interlocking relationship.

## Finding the Balance: Data Is a Compass, Not a Map

Every technique covered in this course — anomaly detection, RFM, image-similarity recommendation, SEO, UX analysis, generative AI — is a tool for understanding "what already exists" more clearly and operating it more efficiently. Imagining "what doesn't exist yet," on the other hand, still belongs to people. The ideal approach in practice isn't choosing one over the other, but building a cycle: intuition proposes a direction, data validates that direction's soundness, and the validation results feed back into the next intuitive judgment. The spiral workflow model covered in Chapter 2 of this course can, in the end, be seen as exactly this kind of repeating cycle, structured into the practice of data analysis.

## Chapter Summary

- Cherry-picking is a risk that comes not from the data itself, but from the bias of the person selecting which data to show.
- Data is inherently backward-looking, which limits its ability to predict new trends that don't yet exist (Wedel & Kannan, 2016).
- Vivienne Westwood, Issey Miyake, and Virgil Abloh are representative examples of shifting the course of fashion history through intuition, without data.
- As with the metaverse virtual fitting case (Jeon & Kim, 2022), intuitive judgment and data-driven validation in practice work as an interlocking cycle rather than an opposition.
- Every technique covered in this course is ultimately a tool that helps people make better judgments — not a replacement for human intuition.
