# Chapter 9: Image-Similarity Recommendation

You've probably seen a "similar products" section while browsing a fashion store. This chapter covers the logic behind automatically finding products similar to the one a customer is currently viewing, using data made of images paired with descriptions (labels) of those images. Unlike sales data, this time we're working with **images converted into numbers**, and getting good accuracy requires a deep learning algorithm.

> **Something to think about**
> You want to recommend "products similar to this jacket" from a photo of a model wearing a black jacket, but the system instead recommends products similar to the pants the model is wearing. What went wrong?

## Key Terms

- **Bounding box**: Marking off the region being compared within an image using a rectangle.
- **Labeling**: Attaching ground-truth information — category, color, style — to an image.
- **Triplet loss**: A training method that uses a set of three images — an anchor, a similar image, and a different image — to push similar images close together and different images far apart.

## How an Image Becomes Numbers (Recap)

As covered in Chapter 3, an image splits into R, G, and B channels, and the brightness of each channel is expressed as a number between 0 and 1. Dark areas get low numbers; areas close to a pure color get high numbers. Image-similarity recommendation can be thought of as comparing these numericized images to find the ones that sit "close together" once expressed as numbers.

## Where Similarity Recommendation Is Used

Image-similarity recommendation is analysis that picks out products resembling each other among everything a store offers. For example, when a customer is looking at a black jacket, the service shows similar-looking jackets underneath it.

The interesting part is that this service is used **more often on marketplace platforms that carry many brands — like Farfetch or Zigzag — than on stores that manufacture and sell their own products**. A single brand typically puts out at most a couple hundred items in a season, so once split by category, there aren't that many genuinely similar products to work with. Single-brand stores tend to focus instead on **coordination recommendation** — "pants and accessories that go with this jacket" — which is a different concept from similarity recommendation. A marketplace platform aggregating many brands, on the other hand, has far more similar products within any given category, so similarity recommendation actually delivers value there.

Beyond product recommendation, similarity matching is also used to check whether a store carries something similar to a competitor's popular item, or to group popular images from multiple stores together to read the current season's trend. It extends further into image-based search — finding images using an image as the query.

## Building the Dataset: Bounding Boxes and Labeling

Training a similarity model first requires telling the computer exactly "what to compare." This happens in two steps.

1. **Bounding box**: Mark the garment being compared with a rectangle. Skip this step, and you risk the problem described above — wanting jacket recommendations but getting recommendations similar to the model's pants instead.
2. **Labeling**: Attach ground truth matching the classification criterion. For a category-based criterion, a label like "cardigan" is attached; for color, "pink"; for style, something like "schoolgirl look."

Labeling matters because it directly encodes a marketer's intent. That said, people often disagree on labels — the same garment might get called "schoolgirl look" by one person and "princess style" by another. When that happens, teams either pool opinions from several people or defer to an expert, but the more contested an area is, the more it costs to resolve — which is why practice tends to favor criteria that are relatively easy to agree on, like color and category. Put differently, coming up with a genuinely novel classification criterion nobody has tried before can itself be the seed of a new recommendation model.

## Triplet Loss: Close Together, Far Apart

Many principles exist for training a similarity model, but one of the most common is **triplet loss**. The idea is intuitive.

1. Imagine gathering every photo in the same labeled category — cardigans, say — and scattering them across an imaginary plane.
2. Pick one photo as the anchor. Photos judged similar to it are pulled close; photos judged different are pushed far away.
3. Repeat the process, picking one of the far-away photos as a new anchor each time, and similar photos naturally cluster together.
4. Drawing x, y coordinates on the plane fixes each photo's position, and a new photo can be placed according to the learned relationships once it comes in.

Run this process at scale with an AI instead of a human, and the result is an image-similarity recommendation model. The DeepFashion dataset (Liu et al., 2016), frequently cited in fashion image recognition research, is exactly this kind of large-scale labeled image resource used for training models this way.

> **Source**: Liu, Z., Luo, P., Qiu, S., Wang, X., & Tang, X. (2016). DeepFashion: Powering robust clothes recognition and retrieval with rich annotations. *Proceedings of the IEEE Conference on Computer Vision and Pattern Recognition*, 1096–1104.

## Case Study: Matching Chinese Wholesale Photos to Domestic Bestsellers

Image-similarity recommendation has also been applied to sourcing, beyond search and recommendation. One account describes a company that received large batches of finished-product photos from Chinese wholesalers to sell on platforms like Zigzag and Coupang, where a merchandiser had to look through ten to twenty thousand photos one by one to decide what to list. Beyond how long that took, the selections drifted over time toward whatever matched that one merchandiser's personal taste. To solve this, the company reportedly collected bestseller images from popular domestic stores and built a feature that used an image-similarity algorithm to find and recommend the wholesale photos most similar to them. The case shows that similarity recommendation can be used not only as a "screen shown to customers" but also as a tool for automating internal work.

## Chapter Summary

- Image-similarity recommendation compares images numericized through RGB to find images that sit close together.
- It's more effective on marketplace platforms carrying many similar products from different brands than on a single brand with a limited product range.
- Building training data requires a bounding box to specify what's being compared, plus labeling according to the chosen classification criterion.
- Triplet loss is a training method that repeatedly pulls similar images close together and pushes different images far apart.
- Similarity recommendation can automate internal work, such as sourcing, and not just customer-facing services.
