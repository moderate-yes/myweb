# Chapter 1: What Is Online Consumer Behavior Data

The traces of decisions and actions that consumers leave behind while recognizing, searching for, purchasing, and evaluating a product are called **consumer behavior data**. Companies record and analyze these traces to feed decisions as quickly as possible. Offline shoppers leave traces too, but the online environment makes recording far easier and processing far more immediate, so the depth and variety of online data go well beyond what any physical store can capture.

> **Something to think about**
> One customer clicked a product online but never bought it; another tried on a garment in a store and walked out. How different is the amount and quality of information a company can gather about these two customers?

## Key Terms

- **Structured data**: Data such as transaction records or demographic information that is easy to organize into tables.
- **Behavioral log data**: Records of clicks, dwell time, and navigation paths collected through tags embedded in a website or app.
- **UGC (User Generated Content)**: Content consumers create voluntarily, such as reviews or outfit photos posted on social media.
- **Geofencing**: A location-based technology that sets a virtual boundary on a map using GPS, Wi-Fi, and cellular data, and automatically sends a message when a device enters or leaves that boundary.

## An Overview of Data Types

Online consumer data spans a wide spectrum depending on its form and how hard it is to collect. At one end sit structured data such as transactions and demographics, already organized into tables. At the other end sit data that need processing, such as text and images, and beyond that, data that are difficult to collect at all without special equipment, such as biometric rhythms or emotional states. Even for the same consumer, a complete picture only emerges when these different layers of data are overlaid.

## Demographic and Transaction Data

The most basic data are structured records that accumulate naturally while running a store: membership sign-up information, purchase history, shopping-cart data. This kind of data alone can enable surprisingly precise predictions.

## Online Behavioral Log Data

Embedding tags in a website or app makes it possible to collect a visitor's location, dwell time, clicks, and other decision and behavior patterns. This kind of log data is mainly used to improve UX; the Korean startup Wisely is known for analyzing log data separately for its main page, store page, product detail page, and custom landing pages.

Several tools are used to collect and analyze log data, and the two most common — GA4 (Google Analytics 4) and Amplitude — differ quite a bit in character.

| Item | Amplitude | GA4 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Data sampling | Full analysis, no sampling | Sampling kicks in beyond 500,000 sessions |
| Retention analysis | Precise D1/D7/D30 cohort comparison | Simple revisit rate only |
| Funnel analysis | Flexible order, tracks paths after drop-off | Fixed order, hard to trace post-drop-off paths |
| Session replay | Supports recording and replaying user sessions | Not supported |

D1, D7, and D30 are retention metrics measuring the share of users who return one day, seven days, and thirty days after signing up. GA4, which is free to start with, is widely used, but teams that need precise cohort comparisons or session replay often move to a paid tool like Amplitude.

## Comments, Reviews, and UGC Data

Comments and reviews attached to products are text data consumers leave voluntarily. As AI technology has become commercialized, the barrier to automatically extracting and classifying key themes in reviews — size, satisfaction, shipping — has dropped, and fashion companies have rapidly adopted data-driven services as a result. The athleisure brand Andar is known for aggregating size-related comments from customers to suggest sizes to future buyers.

Beyond reviews, all the content consumers voluntarily create — including outfit photos on social media — is called UGC. Because content platforms and expression styles differ between countries, analyzing domestic social data in Korea typically relies on tools built specifically for that environment, such as Sometrend or Cre.ma Review.

## Location and Eye-Tracking Data

Location data can connect offline stores with online promotions. Burger King's 2018 "Whopper Detour" campaign is a frequently cited example: customers within 180 meters of a McDonald's location could order a Whopper through the Burger King app for one cent, a promotion built on geofencing technology.

Eye-tracking data captures a customer's unconscious interests and is steadily expanding beyond online UX analysis into optimizing product displays and store layout offline. Because the equipment and experimental design are costly, it is still used more for research than for large-scale operations.

## When Data Piles Up: Building an In-House Analytics Stack

Companies with large customer bases eventually find off-the-shelf analytics tools limiting and move toward building their own data warehouse and analytics stack (Raw DB → SQL → Python → Dashboard). This speeds up decision-making and enables deeper analysis, but it also brings the cost of integrating fragmented data and questions of data sovereignty — where data lives and in what form. This trade-off connects to the "cost-efficiency review" discussed later in this chapter series.

## Chapter Summary

- Consumer behavior data is the record of decisions and actions left across the whole cycle of recognizing, searching for, buying, and evaluating a product.
- The online environment produces far more varied data than offline, thanks to easier recording and real-time processing.
- Data ranges across several layers with different forms and collection difficulty: structured transaction data, behavioral logs, reviews/UGC, location, and eye-tracking.
- Even for the same goal — say, improving UX — different data types call for different tools, such as GA4, Amplitude, Sometrend, or Cre.ma Review.
- As customer scale grows, building an in-house analytics stack becomes an option, but it brings its own cost and data-sovereignty questions.
