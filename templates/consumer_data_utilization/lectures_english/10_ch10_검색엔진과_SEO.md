# Chapter 10: Search Engines and SEO

Getting an online store to show up well in search results starts with understanding how a search engine actually works. This chapter covers how a search engine gathers and organizes web pages before serving them as results, and SEO (Search Engine Optimization) — the practice of optimizing your content so it surfaces near the top of that process.

> **Something to think about**
> You search "wedding guest outfit" and the page you click is filled with stock market content. How would you react? Why does an experience like that become a problem from the search engine operator's point of view?

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this unit, you will be able to:

- Apply SEO to real store operations and use keyword-research tools.

## Key Terms

- **Crawling (in a search engine)**: The process by which a search engine automatically collects a website's content using bots.
- **Indexing**: The process of analyzing collected web pages and organizing them into a searchable database.
- **Snippet**: The title, description, and URL preview shown on a search engine results page (SERP).

## Three Stages of a Search Engine: Crawl → Index → Rank

A search engine operator collects websites with crawler bots (**Crawling**), analyzes what it collected and organizes it into a searchable form (**Indexing**), then pulls and displays results from that organized database whenever a search query comes in (**Ranking & Serving**).

During crawling, what a search engine actually collects is the page's URL, the content on the site — text, images, video — and the code that makes up the page itself. A URL like `https://www.macys.com/shop/b/wool-suit/Color_normal/Gray?id=72490`, for instance, gets split into a domain (`www.macys.com`), a folder path (`shop/b/wool-suit`), and a query (`Color_normal/Gray?id=72490`) that all feed into indexing.

## Indexing Basics: Title, Meta, and Head Tags

Three code elements matter especially at the indexing stage.

**The `<title>` tag** shouldn't contain text unrelated to the page's content, should avoid generic filler like "Untitled" or "Page 1," and shouldn't be too long.

**The `<meta>` tag** should run roughly 150 characters in English (about 75 in Korean), contain different content on each page, and avoid pasting in a page's entire content unedited or writing an overly generic description like "this is a web page."

**The `<head>` tag** shouldn't be used simply to emphasize text within a paragraph rather than as an actual subheading, and shouldn't be overused or applied to overly long content.

Search engines don't disclose their full indexing criteria. Google alone has published over 250 rules, and even those change frequently and represent only a partial disclosure. In the end, "producing genuinely good content" is a more fundamental approach than trying to memorize every search-engine rule one by one.

## What Content Search Engines Favor

Search engine operators generally want to rank pages that are: information-rich, have long dwell time, have a low bounce rate, are well-structured, accurately reflect search intent, are backed by trustworthy sources, and load fast while being mobile-optimized.

A few of these are easy to misread. Longer dwell time is good, but an extremely long dwell time can look like a screen just left open. Longer content looks more informative, but search engines also check for repeated words to filter out pages that only pad their length by repeating the same phrase. A page mixing images with text, structured with headers and links, is rated as "well-structured." And a page that immediately buries a click in ads, causing visitors to bounce right back out, sees its future ranking suffer.

The title, description, and URL preview shown on a search results page is called a **snippet**. Beyond the regular snippet, there are table-format snippets, paragraph snippets, and rich snippets that also display star ratings, prices, and similar details.

## Applying SEO to Running a Store

Store-building platforms like Cafe24, Imweb, and Wix offer some automatic optimization, but there are still things an operator has to handle directly.

- **Category and product classification**: Build a category structure that matches search intent.
- **Product name registration**: Include words people are likely to search for in the product name (covered in more detail below).
- **Search settings**: Fill in every search-exposure setting the platform offers.
- **Detailed description**: Provide thorough, specific information so customers stay on the page after clicking.
- **Product images**: Use images that draw a click.

## Keyword Research: Searching "Wedding Guest Outfit"

The goal of keyword analysis is to find keywords that are both highly relevant to what you're selling and currently drawing attention, then invest in search ads around them. The process runs roughly like this:

1. Decide on core keywords based on the business goal.
2. Expand related keywords with a research tool — Google Keyword Planner, Item Scout, Panda Rank, and Twinword Ideas are examples used for this.
3. Settle on final target keywords by weighing the statistics against the business goal.

Searching "wedding guest outfit" (하객룩) in Google Keyword Planner and sorting by bid price, for instance, surfaces detailed sub-keywords combining season or size — things like "July wedding guest," "summer wedding guest," "plus size," "size 77." A keyword like "pants," which anyone might use, is already fiercely contested and commands a high ad price; an overly narrow keyword like "loose pants that don't need a belt" risks being something nobody actually searches for. Finding the balance between those two extremes is the core of keyword selection. After launching, tools like Google Search Console or Naver Search Advisor show which keywords actually drove traffic, letting you adjust strategy from there.

The product-name word count results from Chapter 6 can double as a starting point for this keyword research — words that appear often in popular product names are likely already words that search well.

## Chapter Summary

- A search engine runs in three stages: crawling (collecting), indexing (organizing), and ranking (serving).
- Title, meta, and head tags each have rules to avoid when writing them, and a search engine's full criteria are only ever partially disclosed.
- Search engines favor content that is information-rich, has a reasonably long dwell time, a low bounce rate, and is well-structured.
- Don't rely solely on a store platform's automatic optimization — category, product name, search settings, description, and images still need direct attention.
- Keyword research is the work of finding the balance between an overly common keyword and an overly narrow one.
