# Chapter 13: Where Big-Data Marketing Is Applied, and the Tools for It

Where do techniques like anomaly detection, RFM, image similarity, SEO, and UX analysis — everything covered so far — actually get used in running a real store? This chapter organizes the six representative areas where big-data marketing gets applied, and looks at no-code tools that make these techniques usable even without a data background.

> Think of a shopping app you use often. Behind screen elements like "recommended products," "this much stock at this price," or "how about this item?" — what kind of data analysis might be hiding behind each one?

## Key Terms

- **No-code tool**: Software that enables data collection, analysis, and marketing automation without writing code directly.
- **Dynamic pricing**: A pricing strategy that adjusts prices in real time based on demand, inventory, and competitor pricing.

## Six Areas Where Big-Data Marketing Is Applied

The various analysis techniques covered so far ultimately feed into one or more of the following six areas.

**① Product planning**: Deciding what products to make and what options (color, size) to prepare, based on data. A representative example is finding a "category gap" on Naver Shopping — a category with high search volume but relatively few registered products — and using that gap to plan new products.

**② Pricing**: Dynamic pricing, which adjusts prices in real time based on demand, inventory, and competitor price changes, is the representative example here. Companies running their own private-label (PB) brands, like Musinsa Standard, manage their cost structure closely with data to build price competitiveness.

**③ Search optimization**: This is the SEO and keyword research covered in Chapter 10.

**④ Personalization**: Showing each customer different recommendations and different screens based on their behavior and purchase history. Stitch Fix, the U.S. styling subscription service, is frequently cited as a representative example of data-driven personalization — reportedly combining customer-entered style preferences with actual return and purchase data to inform stylist recommendations.

**⑤ Inventory planning**: Forecasting what products to produce and order, and in what quantity. Inditex, Zara's parent company, is widely known for feeding in-store sales data back to headquarters in near real time to drive fast, small-batch production and restocking — a case frequently cited as emblematic of data-driven inventory management in the fast-fashion industry.

**⑥ Shopping experience (UX)**: This is the A/B testing, funnel analysis, and personalized screen design covered in Chapter 11.

> **Fact-check needed**
> The ways Stitch Fix and Zara/Inditex use data are frequently cited across media and industry sources, but the companies often don't officially disclose the specific data pipelines or figures in detail. When citing these in course materials or reports, it's safer to re-verify them against each company's latest official statements or a credible reporting source.

## Comparing E-Commerce Platforms

If you're planning to run a store yourself, the data tools available to you depend heavily on which platform you choose.

| Platform | Characteristics |
|---|---|
| Naver Smart Store | Tightly integrated with Naver's search and shopping ecosystem; advantageous for leveraging domestic search traffic |
| Cafe24 | A platform with a large share of the domestic store-building market, offering a wide range of app-store-style extensions |
| Imweb | A domestic website-style store builder with high design flexibility |
| Shopify | A global store builder with strengths in overseas expansion and global sales, backed by a rich app ecosystem |

Each platform provides its own built-in analytics tools, but when more sophisticated analysis or marketing automation is needed, stores often integrate the separate no-code tools introduced below.

## A Directory of No-Code Tools by Purpose

Even without a data role, tools like the following can bring you closer to data-driven decision-making.

| Purpose | Example tools | Key functions |
|---|---|---|
| Review management | Cre.ma Review | Automates review collection, display, and photo-review management |
| Personalized recommendations | Laplace | Automates product recommendations based on customer behavior data |
| Marketing automation (CRM) | Bigin | Manages customer data and automatically sends marketing messages |
| Data integration/analysis | Cgro | Integrates and analyzes sales and ad data across multiple channels |
| Keyword research | Item Scout, Panda Rank | Keyword trend analysis, as covered in Chapter 10 |
| Website analytics | GA4, Amplitude | Visitor behavior log analysis, as covered in Chapter 1 |

What these tools have in common is that they let you connect data and receive analysis results with just a few clicks on a dashboard, without writing code yourself. That said, the skill of interpreting "why" a tool's automatically generated numbers look the way they do still falls to a human — and the concepts covered throughout this course (RFM, funnels, anomaly detection, and so on) are exactly the foundation for that interpretation.
