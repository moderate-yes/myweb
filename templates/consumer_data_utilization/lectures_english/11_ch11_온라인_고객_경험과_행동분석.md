# Chapter 11: Online Customer Experience and Behavioral Analytics

Whether a customer who arrives through search actually goes on to buy something is an entirely different question. This chapter starts with the difference between UI and UX, then covers the data used to measure online customer experience, representative analysis methods like A/B testing, funnel analysis, and cohort analysis, and how real fashion platforms turn this data into on-screen features.

## Key Terms

- **UI (User Interface)**: The screen and interaction elements that mediate between a user and a system.
- **UX (User Experience)**: The total experience a user feels and thinks while using a product or service.
- **Funnel analysis**: Analysis that breaks the path to conversion into stages to find the friction points where users drop off instead of moving forward.
- **Cohort analysis**: Analysis that tracks a group of users sharing a common trait over time.

## UI and UX

**UI** is the mediator between a user and a system — the visible digital interface, whether it's a website, a mobile app, or a smart car's dashboard. **UX** isn't limited to visual elements; it's the whole strategic process of identifying and solving the problems a user runs into while using a service. Brands try to weave UX into UI.

## Why Online Customer Experience Matters

An online customer almost never gets a "second chance." Slow loading or failing to find the information they wanted sends them straight to another site. On the flip side, a single button or a single line of copy can drive a huge change. Fashion platforms have moved past simply "displaying products" and now put real effort into designing a screen customers actually want to spend time browsing.

## Data Used in UX Analysis

UX analysis draws on both the transaction and customer data a store needs to operate, and behavioral and service data collected purely for analysis.

- **Transaction data**: Who bought what, and when.
- **Customer data**: Sign-up information like address and gender, membership activity history, CS inquiry content.
- **Customer behavior data**: Clicks, page-view logs, page dwell time, cart contents.
- **Customer service data**: Customer preference data, biometric rhythm data, and similar.

## Four Representative Analysis Methods

| Method | Purpose |
|---|---|
| A/B Testing | Verify which design performs better |
| Heatmap Analysis | Identify where users focus their attention |
| Funnel Analysis | Find where users drop off at each stage |
| Cohort Analysis | Track how user groups change over time |

**A/B testing** shows two versions to real users and compares performance. Booking.com, the global travel platform, is often cited as a company with an experimentation culture so strong that it once ran more than 1,000 A/B tests simultaneously. The person who led its experimentation culture named four elements required to sustain a healthy A/B testing practice: **transparency** — publishing every experiment in a searchable, shared repository; **peer review** — team members reviewing each other's experiments; **tolerance for failure** — leaders treating failure as a learning opportunity; and **open-mindedness** — being willing to accept that your own judgment could be wrong. A/B testing looks methodologically simple, but it's hard to sustain without a culture like this behind it.

**Funnel analysis** visualizes the path a user takes toward conversion (a purchase, for example) stage by stage, to find the friction points where they fail to move on to the next step. Finding a stage where the conversion rate (the share of users moving from the current stage to the next) drops sharply lets a team design an A/B test targeted at exactly that point. That said, funnel analysis alone can't always explain the cause, so it sometimes needs to be paired with follow-up qualitative analysis.

**Cohort analysis** splits users into groups by a shared trait — sign-up timing or a specific behavior — and tracks how that group changes over time. Comparing **retention** — the share of users who return after their first visit — at D1 (the day after signup), D7 (seven days later), and D30 (thirty days later) is a typical example. Completely different insights emerge depending on how the group is defined, which makes that definition the single biggest factor in whether the analysis succeeds.

**Heatmap analysis** visualizes, using color, where users look at and click most on a screen — making it easy to see at a glance which areas draw attention and which get ignored.

## Behavioral Data in Recommendation and Ranking

Amazon's A9 algorithm is said to fold real behavioral data — clicks, purchases, dwell time, conversion rate, sales velocity, reviews — into search ranking as a core signal, surfacing not simply the product matching a keyword but "the product customers actually responded to." Fashion vertical platforms like Musinsa define exposure ranking similarly, based on consumer behavior data. Because the time a consumer spends inside an app is itself treated as an important metric, platforms also experiment with categorizing behavioral traits in multiple ways to drive recommendations from several angles at once.

## Ad Targeting and Privacy

Consumer behavior data is also used to narrow ad targeting precisely — but this use is always in tension with privacy regulation. The ad-tech company Criteo is known to have received a final penalty from CNIL, France's data protection authority, in 2023, with a fine of 40 million euros (reduced from an initially proposed 60 million euros). This illustrates how, as personalized marketing grows, consumer trust and privacy protection become a core long-term asset rather than a side concern.

## UX Improvements Built Into Real Screens

Domestic fashion platforms turn UX data into concrete screen features. Common examples include breaking filters into finer categories — as seen on Farfetch and Naver Shopping — so customers can narrow products by exactly the conditions they want; surfacing comments prominently on the product detail screen itself, as Andar and Zigzag do; and placing a "wishlist" button before the cart step, as Musinsa and Zigzag do, to hold onto customers who aren't ready to commit to a purchase yet. Diversifying recommendation methods — showing products from several angles at once, such as recently viewed, frequently bought together, and bought by customers with similar taste — is another common approach.

## Chapter Summary

- UI is the screen connecting a user to a system; UX is the total experience of using that screen.
- Online customers rarely get a second chance, so even a seemingly minor button or line of copy can make a large difference.
- A/B testing, heatmaps, funnel analysis, and cohort analysis each serve a different purpose, and A/B testing in particular is hard to sustain without an organizational culture to support it.
- Platforms like Amazon's A9 and Musinsa fold real behavioral data into search and recommendation ranking.
- Ad targeting built on behavioral data is in tension with privacy regulation, and consumer trust becomes a long-term asset as a result.
